Physical Profile

Size Range: 7.5 inches (19 cm)

Identification Markings: Fiji banded iguanas are bright green lizards with crested spines and long tails, which help them balance while climbing trees. Males have wide blue or light green stripes along their body. Females, on the other hand, are generally solid green but may have a few white or pale blue spots. These lizards have reddish-orange eyes and yellow nostrils.

Habitat & Geography

Habitat(s): These lizards tend to prefer the plants found in wet forests. They are arboreal, spending most of their time in trees. Therefore, they are found in areas with high vegetation and trees at least 20 feet (6 meters) tall. Like other iguanas, they are also strong swimmers.

Distribution: Fiji banded iguanas are only found on the Fijian islands. They are most prevalent on the central and northwestern islands, generally between 656 and 1,640 feet (200 and 500 meters) above sea level. They live in the mesic to moist forests of Viti Levu, Vanua Levu, Ovalau, Viwa, Kadavu and their associated islands.

Ecology & Behavior

Feeding and Diet

They are primarily herbivorous and forage for leaves, flowers and fruit. On a rare occasion, they may also eat insects.

Behaviors and Adaptations

Most of the iguana's day is spent in the trees basking in the sunlight. The Fiji banded iguana is diurnal, spending part of each day foraging, basking, and watching over their territory.

Communication

When the highly visual male comes across another male, he will aggressively defend his territory by bobbing its head in a warning. The more excited he is, the faster his head moves. He will deepen his green coloration to intensify his bands. He will attack with an open mouth and possibly expand the dewlap to appear larger.

Life History Cycle & Breeding Behaviors

Fiji banded iguanas reach reproductive maturity at 3 to 4 years old. Males court females by bobbing their heads and flicking their tongues. After copulation, females dig burrows in nesting sites to lay their eggs. They generally lay a clutch of about five eggs, which incubate for seven to nine months.

Predators

Predators are the black rat, Indian mongoose, and feral cats

Physical Profile

Size Range:2'6" - 2'9"

Identification Markings: Blue-throated macaws have vibrant turquoise-blue feathers covering their throat, head, back, and the upper parts of their wings and tail. A stripe of golden-yellow feathers is seen between the top of their head and on the sides of their face. This golden-yellow coloration goes to the underside of their body, wings, and tail. Their face has a sparsely feathered skin patch near the base of their dark beak, with five or six horizontal blue feather stripes unique to each bird, which can help identify adults. Newly hatched macaws are pink and featherless, developing gray down before their colored feathers emerge.

Habitat & Geography

Habitat(s): Blue-throated macaws live, eat, and nest in trees. Their main way of getting around is flying, but they can also climb trees, move along branches, and walk on the ground. These birds are active during the day and usually stay in one general area. However, occasional sightings in Paraguay suggest they sometimes travel long distances.

Distribution: native to the palm forests of Bolivia

Ecology & Behavior

Feeding and Diet

Blue-throated macaws don't eat as many seeds and nuts as other macaws do. They mostly eat fruit from big palm trees. These macaws eat the fleshy part of ripe fruit. Sometimes, they have also been seen drinking the juices from very young fruit.

Behaviors and Adaptations

Blue-throated macaws are social birds as they form monogamous mating pairs and will also live in small groups. One group of 70 individuals was known at one point, but this species is not often seen in large flocks as other species of macaws have. This is most likely because blue-throated macaws are very rare and have such a low population that the formation of such large groups is very unlikely.

Communication

Blue-throated macaws are social birds, living in small groups. Mating pairs are very affectionate, often preening each other's feathers and sitting close together. These macaws are also sometimes seen interacting with blue-and-yellow macaws.

Life History Cycle & Breeding Behaviors

A mating pair of blue-throated macaws can be very affectionate towards one another and are often seen preening each other's feathers and perching close together.

Predators

Adults can be preyed upon by great horned owls , southern caracaras, coati, tayras, and brown capuchin monkeys. Nestlings are preyed upon by crane hawks, while both eggs and nestlings are eaten by toco toucans.

Physical Profile

Size Range: 81–91 cm (32–36 in) in length; weighs 1.0–1.5 kg (2–3 lb).

Identification Markings: Bright aqua blue feathers on the top of the body, lime-colored head, and a rich, deep yellow/light orange underside. Features a black beak, black feathers under the chin, gray feet with black talons, and bare white facial skin with sparse black feathers forming a striped pattern around pale light yellow eyes.

Habitat & Geography

Habitat(s): Swampy tropical and subtropical forests, seasonally flooded forests, riparian areas, palm savannas, and urban environments.

Distribution: Widespread throughout most of Brazil and its bordering northern and western countries, spanning from Bolivia to French Guiana.

Physical Profile

Size Range: Females average an 18-inch (46 cm) head-body length with an 18-inch (46 cm) tail. Males reach around 28 inches (71 cm) head-body length with a tail extending up to 35 inches (89 cm).

Identification Markings: Shiny black coat paired with a long, white mantle of fur that frames the small, round face in a "blowout" style. Long plumes of white hair fall down their sides like epaulets. Notably lacks cheek pouches.

Habitat & Geography

Habitat(s): Forest canopies across dense rainforests, lowland, bamboo, coastal, and montane ecosystems, as well as savannas and swamplands.

Distribution: Central Africa, ranging from Gabon and Cameroon in the west to Ethiopia in the east, and extending southward to Tanzania.

Ecology & Behavior

Feeding and Diet

Primarily folivorous, consuming over 45 different species of leaves, stems, bark, buds, shoots, and insects. They spend most daylight hours foraging to consume up to 7 pounds (3 kg) of leaves daily.

Behaviors and Adaptations

Highly arboreal and diurnal. They feature a specialized three-chambered stomach containing unique bacteria to ferment and digest cellulose-rich, toxic, or unripe plant material, eliminating food competition. They can leap up to 50 feet (15.2 meters) through the canopy, utilizing their lightweight bones, balancing tail, and a parachute-like mantle of fur.

Communication

Dominant males vocalize loud roars in the morning to reinforce territorial boundaries. Males also emit warning roars when threatened by predators to signal the troop to disperse.

Life History Cycle & Breeding Behaviors

Polygynous social structure where dominant males maintain control over reproductive females. Mating occurs year-round, peaking in the rainy season, with births concentrated in September and October. Following a gestation of 147 to 178 days, a single offspring (or rarely twins) is born. Mothers practice co-parenting within the troop, weaning occurs at 15 months, and sexual maturity is reached at two years for females and four years for males.

Predators

Natural predators include leopards and eagles. Humans act as predators to the macaws via hunting for the pet trade, food, and feathers, while colobus monkeys disperse rapidly when threatened by their primary canopy threats.
