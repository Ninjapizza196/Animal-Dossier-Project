Physical Profile

Size Range: 7.5 inches (19 cm)[1]

Identification Markings: Fiji banded iguanas are bright green lizards with crested spines and long tails, which help them balance while climbing trees. Males have wide blue or light green stripes along their body. Females, on the other hand, are generally solid green but may have a few white or pale blue spots[2]. These lizards have reddish-orange eyes and yellow nostrils[1].

Habitat & Geography

Habitat(s): These lizards tend to prefer the plants found in wet forests. They are arboreal, spending most of their time in trees[1]. Therefore, they are found in areas with high vegetation and trees at least 20 feet (6 meters) tall[5]. Like other iguanas, they are also strong swimmers[1].

Distribution: Fiji banded iguanas are only found on the Fijian islands[2]. They are most prevalent on the central and northwestern islands, generally between 656 and 1,640 feet (200 and 500 meters) above sea level[3]. They live in the mesic to moist forests of Viti Levu, Vanua Levu, Ovalau, Viwa, Kadavu and their associated islands[4].

Ecology & Behavior

Feeding and Diet: They are primarily herbivorous and forage for leaves, flowers and fruit[1]. On a rare occasion, they may also eat insects[5].

Behaviors and Adaptations: Most of the iguana's day is spent in the trees basking in the sunlight. The Fiji banded iguana is diurnal, spending part of each day foraging, basking, and watching over their territory[3].

Communication: When the highly visual male comes across another male, he will aggressively defend his territory by bobbing its head in a warning. The more excited he is, the faster his head moves. He will deepen his green coloration to intensify his bands. He will attack with an open mouth and possibly expand the dewlap to appear larger[3].

Life History

Cycle & Breeding Behaviors: Fiji banded iguanas reach reproductive maturity at 3 to 4 years old[5]. Males court females by bobbing their heads and flicking their tongues[3]. After copulation, females dig burrows in nesting sites to lay their eggs. They generally lay a clutch of about five eggs, which incubate for seven to nine months[1].

Predators: Predators are the black rat, Indian mongoose, and feral cats[4].

Size Range: 2'6" - 2'9"[6]

Identification Markings: Blue-throated macaws have vibrant turquoise-blue feathers covering their throat, head, back, and the upper parts of their wings and tail. A stripe of golden-yellow feathers is seen between the top of their head and on the sides of their face. This golden-yellow coloration goes to the underside of their body, wings, and tail[8]. Their face has a sparsely feathered skin patch near the base of their dark beak, with five or six horizontal blue feather stripes unique to each bird, which can help identify adults. Newly hatched macaws are pink and featherless, developing gray down before their colored feathers emerge[9].

Habitat & Geography

Habitat(s): Blue-throated macaws live, eat, and nest in trees[6]. Their main way of getting around is flying, but they can also climb trees, move along branches, and walk on the ground[9]. These birds are active during the day and usually stay in one general area[7]. However, occasional sightings in Paraguay suggest they sometimes travel long distances[9].

Distribution: Native to the palm forests of Bolivia[8].

Size Range: 81–91 cm (32–36 in) in length; weighs 1.0–1.5 kg (2–3 lb)[10].

Identification Markings: Bright aqua blue feathers on the top of the body, lime-colored head, and a rich, deep yellow/light orange underside. Features a black beak, black feathers under the chin, gray feet with black talons, and bare white facial skin with sparse black feathers forming a striped pattern around pale light yellow eyes[10].

Ecology & Behavior

Feeding and Diet: Blue-and-yellow macaws mainly eat seeds, nuts, and fruits. They use their strong beaks to break open nut shells and to crush seeds. In some cases, they consume clay found at riverbanks which allows them to digest the toxins from unripe seeds that they may have ingested[14].

Behaviors and Adaptations: Blue-and-yellow macaws are mainly found in pairs but can congregate in groups to form flocks. When in pairs, they fly close together with their wings almost touching. When foraging they may join small, noisy flocks during the early morning, by midday they begin to search for shade[15]. Blue-and-yellow macaws are extremely wary; at any sign of danger they fly into the air screeching loudly[12].

Communication: Blue-and-yellow macaws communicate by loud vocalizations or flock calls. They also have highly developed visual acuity. They have very complex social behavior and vocalizations, as do all macaws[15].

Life History

Cycle & Breeding Behaviors: Blue-and-yellow macaws reach sexual maturity at 3 to 4 years of age. Their breeding season is during the first half of the year and they breed about every 1 to 2 years[10]. Nests are found high up in tall trees, mainly in cavities already made by other animals[15]. Females lay 2 to 3 eggs and incubate them for 24 to 28 days, after which the young hatch blind and featherless. After 10 days the young begin to develop feathers. Within 3 months fledglings become independent[14].

Predators: Known predators include harpy eagles, hawk eagles and orange-breasted falcons that attack while the birds are in flight[15]. Humans are also predators because they hunt these birds for the pet trade, food, and feathers[13].

Physical Profile

Size Range: Females average an 18-inch (46 cm) head-body length with an 18-inch (46 cm) tail. Males reach around 28 inches (71 cm) head-body length with a tail extending up to 35 inches (89 cm)[16].

Identification Markings: Shiny black coat paired with a long, white mantle of fur that frames the small, round face in a "blowout" style. Long plumes of white hair fall down their sides like epaulets. Notably lacks cheek pouches[16].

Habitat & Geography

Habitat(s): Forest canopies across dense rainforests, lowland, bamboo, coastal, and montane ecosystems, as well as savannas and swamplands[16].

Distribution: Central Africa, ranging from Gabon and Cameroon in the west to Ethiopia in the east, and extending southward to Tanzania[16].

Ecology & Behavior

Feeding and Diet: Primarily folivorous, consuming over 45 different species of leaves, stems, bark, buds, shoots, and insects. They spend most daylight hours foraging to consume up to 7 pounds (3 kg) of leaves daily[16].

Behaviors and Adaptations: Highly arboreal and diurnal. They feature a specialized three-chambered stomach containing unique bacteria to ferment and digest cellulose-rich, toxic, or unripe plant material, eliminating food competition. They can leap up to 50 feet (15.2 meters) through the canopy, utilizing their lightweight bones, balancing tail, and a parachute-like mantle of fur16.

Communication: Dominant males vocalize loud roars in the morning to reinforce territorial boundaries. Males also emit warning roars when threatened by predators to signal the troop to disperse16.

Cycle & Breeding Behaviors: Polygynous social structure where dominant males maintain control over reproductive females. Mating occurs year-round, peaking in the rainy season, with births concentrated in September and October. Following a gestation of 147 to 178 days, a single offspring (or rarely twins) is born. Mothers practice co-parenting within the troop, weaning occurs at 15 months, and sexual maturity is reached at two years for females and four years for males16.

Predators: Natural predators include leopards and eagles. Humans act as predators via hunting for the pet trade, food, and feathers, while colobus monkeys disperse rapidly when threatened by their primary canopy threats16.
